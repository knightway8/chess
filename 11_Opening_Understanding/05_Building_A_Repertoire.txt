Building a coherent repertoire
==============================

A repertoire is a set of prepared choices that leads to positions you understand. It does
not need to cover every named variation with equal depth. It needs an answer to common
structures, forcing lines, and the deviations you encounter.

Choose positions that fit your learning goals and available study time. Open games teach
development and tactics. Closed centers teach maneuvering and pawn breaks. A system can
reduce early branching, but it does not remove the need to respond to the opponent's setup.

For each opening, keep a one-page verbal map: central idea, normal development, main breaks,
tactical warnings, favorable exchanges, and undesirable structures. Keep exact move
sequences in a separate board-capable tool if you want them. This library deliberately stays
free of notation.

Add illustrative games and recurring endgames. If an opening often produces an isolated
central pawn, learn that structure. If it produces opposite-wing castling, study pawn races.
Opening and middlegame study should connect.

Review by retrieval. Close the notes and explain the plan. Solve a decision from the
position. Spaced repetition of choices is more useful than rereading a long file. Delete
lines that never occur and deepen branches that repeatedly cause problems.

Maintain options for both colors. With the first move, know how you handle major defensive
families. With the second move, choose a response to king-pawn, queen-pawn, and flank
starts. Rare openings usually can be met by sound development and central play, but check
concrete traps afterward.

Exercise: make a coverage table containing opening family, intended structure, main break,
and one model game to study. Empty cells show useful next work. A thousand memorized moves
without this map can still leave a repertoire incoherent.
