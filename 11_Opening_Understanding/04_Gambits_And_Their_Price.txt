Gambits and the price of activity
=================================

A gambit offers material, usually a pawn, for development, open lines, central control, or
king exposure. Judge it like an investment. Identify the useful time or access purchased,
the duration of compensation, and the position after the defender returns material.

Accepting is not an obligation. The offered pawn may pull a piece away from development or
open a dangerous line. Declining may preserve a solid center. When accepting, consider
giving the pawn back to complete development or trade queens.

The gambiteer must use time. If the extra development is spent on slow pawn moves, the
material deficit remains while compensation fades. Bring pieces into the attack and stop the
defender from consolidating.

The defender should not cling to every pawn. Complete development, close or exchange
attacking lines, and challenge the center. A safe extra pawn becomes valuable later. An
exposed king with an undeveloped army makes the pawn irrelevant.

Some gambits are theoretically respected; others rely on traps and become doubtful after
accurate defense. Even a dubious gambit can teach development and tactics, but label its
objective status honestly. Surprise value is not the same as soundness.

Study the defender's best quiet line, not only famous disasters. If your opening knowledge
ends when the opponent declines the trap, you have learned a trick rather than an opening.

Exercise: for a gambit, count useful developing tempi gained and identify the defender's
consolidating plan. Then find the moment when the material investment must produce something
lasting.
