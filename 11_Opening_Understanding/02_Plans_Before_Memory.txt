Plans before memory
===================

Opening memory is useful when it preserves reasons and tactical details. Memory without
understanding collapses when the opponent changes the order. Learn each opening through its
typical pawn structure, main breaks, best and worst pieces, king-safety concerns, and common
transition.

Start with a small decision tree. Against the opponent's main setup, know one reliable
development plan. Against an early central challenge, know whether to exchange, advance, or
defend. Against a flank attack, know the central reaction. Expand the tree from games you
actually play.

For every memorized move, explain what it prevents and what would happen after the natural
alternative. If no explanation exists, mark the move for study rather than pretending it is
known. Some move-order details are tactical and may not compress into a simple principle;
those require exact board practice.

Study model games for recurring plans. One short win can teach a trap, but a range of games
shows what happens after sound defense. Include wins, losses, and draws by both colors. The
losses reveal typical overreach and difficult endings.

Review the first moment you were outside preparation. Did you recognize the structure? Did
you spend too much time trying to remember an unavailable line? Use that position to grow
your notes. A repertoire should develop from encountered decisions, not from collecting
endless variations.

Do not confuse a named variation with a complete plan. Names can separate positions
differing by one move order. Transpositions can lead different names to the same structure.
Learn the position you have, including any rights or tactical changes created by the route.

Exercise: choose one opening and write five facts without notation: the central tension,
your main pawn break, your best piece, your usual problem piece, and the endgame you
welcome. If you cannot, learn those before adding moves.
