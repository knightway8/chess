Move order and transposition
============================

Move order determines which options each side permits. Two routes may reach the same final
arrangement, but one route may allow an intermediate attack, force a concession, or remove
castling rights. A move order is therefore a negotiation over possibilities.

A flexible move keeps several structures available. Flexibility has value only when the
alternatives are good and you know when to choose them. Delaying a central commitment can
invite the opponent to seize space. Committing early can prevent a specific setup but reveal
your plan.

Transposition occurs when different sequences reach the same complete position. This is
common among queen-pawn and flank openings. It allows knowledge to transfer: the pawn breaks
and piece roles of the final structure matter more than the label used on the first turn.

Watch for tactical differences. If one route develops a defender before opening the center,
a sacrifice may fail; if another delays it, the sacrifice may work. If a king or rook moved
and returned, castling rights differ despite visual similarity. The player to move also
changes the evaluation.

When an opponent uses an unfamiliar order, ask what they omitted. Did they delay
development, leave a pawn unsupported, or simply preserve a useful option? Punish only a
real defect. Trying to refute every unusual order can lead to overextension.

Build repertoire notes by position and decision, not only by opening name. Group
transpositions that share structure. Add a warning where similar-looking positions require
different tactics.

Exercise: compare two games with the same pawn structure reached from different openings.
Identify which pieces differ and whether the difference changes a main break. This is
practical transposition study.
