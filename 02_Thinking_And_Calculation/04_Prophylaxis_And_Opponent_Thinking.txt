Prophylaxis and thinking from the other side
============================================

Prophylaxis is preparation against the opponent's useful ideas. It is broader than
preventing an immediate threat. A move can stop a freeing pawn break, preserve a bishop
retreat, deny a knight outpost, or make an intended exchange unattractive. The best
preventive moves often improve your own position at the same time.

Begin by temporarily adopting the opponent's perspective. Which of their pieces is worst?
Which exchange would relieve their pressure? Which pawn break would activate their army?
Where would their queen like to enter? You are not trying to guess their personality. You
are identifying the resources that the position makes available.

Then ask whether those ideas are actually good. Preventing a harmless plan wastes time. If
an enemy pawn advance weakens their king and does not create a real threat, allowing it may
be better than stopping it. Prophylaxis requires evaluation, not fear. The opponent's
apparent wish and the opponent's best move are not necessarily the same.

Consider a position where your opponent has a cramped bishop and one central pawn break that
would free it. You could launch an immediate wing attack, but that might give the opponent
time to open the center. A quiet rook move that increases pressure on the central pawn may
make the freeing break tactically impossible. Your later attack becomes stronger because the
defender's main source of activity has been restricted.

Preventive thinking is also useful when winning. Before collecting another pawn, ask what
could make conversion difficult: perpetual check, a blockade, a passed pawn, or a fortress.
You may prefer to stop the opponent's only active idea first. This is not excessive caution
if the advantage is stable and the preventive move costs little. It is a way to make the
position easier to handle.

The danger is trying to prevent everything. Every move allows some possibilities and removes
others. If you spend all your time responding to remote threats, you can surrender the
initiative without cause. Rank the opponent's ideas by urgency and effectiveness. Prevent
the important one, tolerate the harmless one, and answer the speculative one only if it
becomes real.

A useful question before any irreversible move is: what does this give the opponent that
they did not have before? A pawn advance may create an outpost. A bishop exchange may remove
control of a color complex. A king move may lose castling rights. The move may still be
best, but recognizing the concession allows you to plan around it.

Practice after each opponent move in a slow game: state their strongest next idea in one
sentence. After the game, compare your prediction with an analysis of the position. The goal
is not perfect prediction of the move they actually chose. The goal is noticing the best
resource they could have chosen, including resources they themselves missed.
