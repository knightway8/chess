Evaluation, uncertainty, and choosing between plans
===================================================

An evaluation is a judgment about the position under good play. It is not a description of
how comfortable you feel or how often similar-looking attacks have worked against your
friends. Separate objective features from practical difficulty. A position can be
objectively defensible and still unpleasant to defend; it can be objectively winning and
still require accuracy you have not yet learned.

Compare lasting and temporary features. Extra material, a stable outpost, and fixed pawn
weaknesses may persist. A lead in development, an exposed king before castling, and a
temporary pin may disappear quickly. A side with a temporary advantage often needs energetic
action. A side with a stable advantage can sometimes improve patiently, provided the
opponent has no active escape.

Do not count features as though each has one vote. Three small advantages can be outweighed
by one decisive danger to the king. A weak pawn may be irrelevant if it cannot be attacked.
A bishop pair may be powerful in an open position and restricted behind locked pawns.
Evaluation means understanding interactions and importance, not adding a checklist
mechanically.

When comparing plans, imagine the opponent cooperating as little as possible. A plan to
attack a backward pawn is weak if the pawn can safely advance. A plan to invade an open file
is incomplete if every entry square is controlled. A plan to win with an extra pawn must
account for blockade and counterplay. Ask what the plan requires and whether those
requirements can be secured.

Use verbal evaluations that explain themselves. 'Slightly better' is less useful than 'My
safer king and active rooks give me easier play, but the opponent can equalize if they
complete the central break.' The second description identifies both the advantage and the
opponent's remedy. It tells you what to do next and what to watch for.

Some decisions remain uncertain. You may be unable to prove whether a sacrifice gives enough
compensation. Compare the reliable alternative with the speculative one, taking account of
the clock and your understanding of the resulting positions. Uncertainty does not make the
sacrifice wrong. It does mean that you should not describe it afterward as forced if you
played it mainly on judgment.

Avoid outcome bias. If an attack succeeds because the opponent misses an easy defense, the
success does not prove the sacrifice sound. If a sound plan fails because you later blunder,
the plan was not necessarily bad. Review the decision at the moment it was made, using the
information and alternatives available then.

Exercise: choose one game where you won from a worse position and one where you lost from a
better position. Identify the evaluation before the turning point and the event that changed
it. This teaches the difference between having an advantage, preserving it, and converting
it. Those are related skills, but they are not the same skill.
