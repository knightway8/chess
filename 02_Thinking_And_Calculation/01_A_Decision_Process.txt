A decision process you can actually use
=======================================

A useful thinking routine must be short enough to survive an ordinary game. Begin by asking
what the opponent's last move changed. It may have created a threat, removed a defender,
opened a line, weakened a square, or quietly prepared a pawn break. Even a retreat can be
dangerous if it clears a route for another piece. Look at the whole change, not only the
moved piece.

Next check urgent matters. Are you in check? Is there a direct mating threat? Is an
important piece attacked? Is something tactically loose? An urgent threat does not always
require passive defense: a stronger counter-threat can work. But you must demonstrate why it
works. Ignoring danger because your own plan feels exciting is not counterplay.

When there is no emergency, make a brief positional inventory. Compare king safety,
material, piece activity, pawn structure, and available pawn breaks. You do not need a long
speech over the board. A sentence such as 'My pieces are active, but the center may open
before my king is safe' already points toward useful candidates. The inventory should
produce decisions rather than become a ritual list.

Generate a small set of candidates with different purposes. One might be forcing, one
defensive, and one improving. The point is not to reach an arbitrary number. The point is to
avoid choosing the first plausible move without comparison. If one move clearly meets an
emergency and the alternatives fail, more candidates are unnecessary. In a complex position,
the first three attractive moves may all miss the important resource.

For each serious candidate, look for the opponent's strongest reply. Search especially for
checks, captures, threats, and changes that undermine your intention. Then continue until
the immediate tactical tension is resolved enough to evaluate. You cannot compare a finished
favorable line with an unfinished unfavorable one fairly. Nor should you stop a line just
before the opponent's obvious recapture.

Before committing, perform a fresh safety check from the position after your intended move.
What will be undefended? Which line will open? What checks will the opponent gain? Is there
a simple capture you overlooked? This final scan is not a complete second calculation. It is
an attempt to catch the error that your preferred story has made invisible.

Time management modifies the routine. With ample time, investigate irreversible decisions
carefully. With seconds left, reduce the process to threats, forcing replies, and a move
that keeps your pieces coordinated. Do not spend all your time creating a perfect thinking
method and then leave no time to execute it.

Practice the routine after a game first. At five moments, write what changed, what was
urgent, which candidates existed, and which reply you missed. The repeated pattern will
reveal your real weakness. Some players fail to notice threats; others notice them but
calculate carelessly; others calculate correctly and evaluate the final position badly.
Different failures require different training.
