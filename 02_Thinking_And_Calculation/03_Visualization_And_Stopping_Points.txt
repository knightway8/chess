Visualization and sensible stopping points
==========================================

Calculation requires holding an imagined position accurately while pieces move within it.
The most common failures are ordinary bookkeeping mistakes: a captured piece remains in the
mental picture, a moved pawn still seems to defend its old square, or a line is imagined
open when a piece still blocks it. Improving visualization means improving the reliability
of that picture, not merely trying to see farther.

Use checkpoints. After a forcing sequence, reconstruct the important surviving pieces, the
king locations, the open lines, and whose turn it is. You do not always need to recite every
pawn. You do need the pieces that affect the conclusion. If your imagined position becomes
uncertain, return to the last reliable checkpoint rather than extending a corrupted line.

Moves change more than destinations. A rook move abandons one file and occupies another. A
knight departure can uncover a bishop. A capture removes a blocker as well as a target. When
mentally playing a move, update both the arrival square and the vacancy it creates. Many
discovered attacks are missed because the analyst tracks only where pieces go.

How far should you calculate? Until you can make a useful judgment, not until you reach a
favorite number of moves. A forcing capture sequence may need several replies before
material is settled. A quiet improving move may be assessed after checking a few dangerous
responses. An ending can require long exact calculation because a single tempo decides
promotion. Depth is a consequence of the position's demands.

A dangerous stopping point is the moment your own threat appears. If you stop immediately
after placing a piece near the king, you have evaluated an intention rather than its
consequences. Give the defender a move. Another dangerous stopping point is an apparently
equal exchange while one side has a powerful intermediate move. Finish the forcing business
before trusting the final count.

Not every line must end in total calm. Some positions remain complex no matter how long a
human calculates. In those cases, distinguish what you know from what you estimate. You may
know that material remains equal and the king escapes the immediate checks, while estimating
that your active pieces offer adequate compensation for weak pawns. This honest division is
better than pretending the whole position is solved.

Train with short sequences you can verify. Read or play a few moves in a separate
board-based study, cover the board, and describe which pieces moved and which lines opened.
Increase length only when the shorter exercise is accurate. For this text library, use the
thought experiments to practice updating relationships without treating their incomplete
descriptions as exact tactical puzzles.

A useful post-game question is whether the mistake came from seeing the wrong position or
judging the right position badly. If you forgot a defender, work on visualization. If you
saw the defender but underestimated a blockade, work on evaluation. Training the wrong skill
can make study feel busy while the same error keeps returning.
