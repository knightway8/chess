Candidate moves and forcing replies
===================================

Checks, captures, and threats deserve early attention because they restrict the opponent's
choices. They do not automatically deserve to be played. A useless check can improve the
enemy king's position, a capture can open a dangerous file, and a one-move threat can be met
while the opponent improves a piece. Use forcing moves as a search priority, not a command.

A candidate is worth calculating when it addresses the position's actual needs. In an
attack, useful candidates may introduce a new attacker or remove a defender. In a cramped
position, a pawn break or exchange may matter more than a direct threat. In an ending, the
critical choice may be a king route or a waiting move. Do not let the word 'tactical' narrow
your imagination to captures.

Generate candidates before becoming emotionally attached to one. If you calculate your
favorite move deeply and then glance at the alternatives, you are comparing unequal amounts
of evidence. A brief initial survey can reveal a quiet move that makes the sacrifice
unnecessary. It can also reveal that the opponent has only one meaningful threat, which
makes a calm defensive move attractive.

Forcing replies are especially important when testing your own move. Imagine that your rook
attacks the enemy queen, but the opponent can give a mating check. The queen attack did not
force the response you expected. The hierarchy of threats depends on consequences. A check
that wins your queen can be stronger than defending their queen; a passed pawn that promotes
with check can reverse an apparently settled exchange.

Look for intermediate moves between expected captures. If you assume every capture will be
immediately recaptured, your analysis will miss many tactics. The opponent may insert a
check, attack a more valuable piece, eliminate a defender, or promote a pawn. After each
exchange, pause mentally and ask whether the side to move has a stronger interruption.

Refutations are valuable. Finding that your intended attack fails to a simple retreat is
progress because it saves you from playing it. Do not treat the discovery as an invitation
to ignore the retreat and search only for a more beautiful continuation. Either repair the
defect, choose another candidate, or establish that the retreat has a concrete drawback.

An efficient comparison often uses one decisive reason to reject a move. If a candidate
loses a rook by force without adequate compensation, there is no need to evaluate its
elegant pawn structure. Spend the saved time on the candidates that survive. Conversely, if
a move is playable but strategically different, compare the resulting positions instead of
declaring one wrong because it does not match your initial plan.

Exercise: review a critical move and list one forcing candidate, one quiet candidate, and
one move that directly answers the opponent's idea. Explain the best objection to each. Your
answer is useful even if two categories lead to the same move. The skill is seeing competing
purposes and testing them fairly.
