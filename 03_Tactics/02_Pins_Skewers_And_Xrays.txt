Pins, skewers, and pressure through pieces
==========================================

A pin restricts a piece because moving it would expose something behind it. An absolute pin
involves the king: the pinned piece cannot legally move in a way that exposes its king to
attack. A relative pin involves a valuable piece or important target. The pinned piece may
legally move, and sometimes moving it with a stronger threat is exactly the right response.

Do not assume every pinned piece is completely frozen. A piece pinned along a line may be
able to move along that same line while continuing to shield the king. It may capture the
pinning piece if the capture is legal. The exact geometry matters. The practical question is
which moves are unavailable, not whether the word 'pinned' applies.

A skewer reverses the usual order of targets. The more urgent target is in front, and moving
it exposes something behind. A rook checking a king on an open line can skewer a rook
farther along that line. A bishop can attack a queen that shields another valuable piece.
The front piece may escape, but the piece behind can remain vulnerable.

An x-ray is pressure along a line through an intervening piece. The attack may become direct
after an exchange or departure. A rook behind an enemy rook can indirectly influence a king
farther down the file. A bishop behind its own knight can become powerful as soon as the
knight moves. Learning to see through blockers helps you predict which exchanges will create
tactics.

Pinned defenders deserve special attention. If a pawn appears to defend a piece but cannot
legally capture without exposing its king, the defense may be ineffective for that exchange.
However, king-movement rules treat attacked squares carefully: a constrained enemy piece can
still make a square unavailable to your king. Do not turn the useful attacking slogan 'the
defender is pinned' into a universal rule about king safety.

To exploit a pin, increase pressure on the restricted piece or attack the target it cannot
adequately defend. A pawn advance can be especially effective because it attacks a more
valuable pinned piece. But first check whether the opponent can break the pin, exchange the
attacker, or create a forcing counterattack. A pin is a source of possibilities, not an
automatic material gain.

To escape a pin, consider moving the valuable target behind the pinned piece, interposing
another defender, challenging the pinning piece, or changing the tactical balance with a
threat. Sometimes the simplest solution is castling or a king move that removes the absolute
pin. Sometimes that is too slow, so a concrete defense is necessary.

Exercise: explain the difference between 'this knight cannot move at all' and 'this knight
cannot leave the line shielding its king.' Then review a game for positions where a
long-range piece was looking through a blocker. Ask what would happen if the blocker
disappeared. That question is often the beginning of a combination.
