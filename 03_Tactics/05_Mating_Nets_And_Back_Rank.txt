Mating nets and back-rank patterns
==================================

An attack becomes a mating net when the king's escape routes are controlled or obstructed
and a decisive check can be delivered. The checking piece is only part of the picture.
Pawns, minor pieces, and even the defender's own army may control or block the squares that
make escape impossible. Strong attackers count exits before celebrating checks.

Back-rank weakness is a common example. A king sheltered behind its own pawns can be
vulnerable to a rook or queen entering along the back rank. The pawns that once provided
shelter may prevent escape. The defender may need an available interposition, a guarding
piece, a trade, or a safe escape square. Merely having a rook on the back rank does not
guarantee safety if it can be deflected or pinned.

Creating an escape square is often useful, but the pawn move must be judged. It can weaken a
diagonal, become a hook for an attack, or cost a crucial tempo. If the opponent already has
a forcing combination, a casual pawn move may be too slow. If your position is stable,
making room for the king can free your heavy pieces for active play.

A smothered mate uses a knight against a king whose own pieces block its escapes. A
bishop-and-rook pattern may combine diagonal control with a rook's checking line. A queen
supported by another piece can sometimes deliver mate close to the king. These descriptions
identify families of geometry. They do not establish mate without the exact position, legal
checks, and escape-square audit.

The attacker's most useful question is often which flight square remains. If the king has
one safe exit, controlling that exit may be stronger than giving another check. A quiet move
can complete the net while a premature check allows escape. This is why attacking play is
not simply an uninterrupted sequence of checks.

The defender should consider giving up material to open an escape route or remove a key
attacker. Losing a rook can be preferable to being mated, and an exchange sacrifice may
eliminate the piece controlling the only exit. Defensive calculation should include king
movement and material concessions, not just attempts to protect everything.

Watch for stalemate when the attack has stripped the opponent of moves. A queen that
controls every escape without checking can turn a win into a draw if the opponent has no
other legal move. When reducing a bare king's space, keep your own king and queen
coordinated and preserve a legal response until the final check is ready.

Training task: after a mating attack, describe the role of every attacking piece. Which one
checked? Which controlled an exit? Which removed a defender? Which blocked a line? This
explanation turns a memorable finish into reusable knowledge. If you remember only the final
checking move, you miss the construction that made it work.
