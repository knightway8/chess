Sacrifices, traps, and practical resistance
===========================================

A sacrifice deliberately gives up material for a more valuable benefit. A tactical sacrifice
may recover the material or force mate through a calculable sequence. A positional sacrifice
may obtain long-term compensation without an immediate recovery. The difference is about the
nature of the justification, not whether the move looks dramatic.

Before sacrificing, name the compensation. Are you exposing the king, obtaining connected
passed pawns, eliminating a blockader, dominating a color complex, or gaining several useful
development moves? Then ask whether the opponent can neutralize that compensation by
returning some material. A sacrifice that works only if the defender greedily keeps
everything may be a trap rather than a sound investment.

The strongest traps are moves you would be willing to play even if the opponent avoids the
trap. They improve your position while giving the opponent a chance to go wrong. A weak trap
sacrifices the quality of your position for a single hoped-for mistake. Such traps may win
occasional fast games but teach dependence on cooperation.

Do not judge an opening by its most famous miniature. Short wins are selected precisely
because someone made a mistake. To assess an opening, examine the opponent's sober defensive
choices and the resulting middlegames. If you dislike every position after correct defense,
the trap is not a reliable repertoire foundation.

When worse, practical resistance is legitimate. Create active threats, keep pieces that can
generate counterplay, and seek positions where the opponent must make decisions. A swindle
is a resource that changes the result after the stronger side errs. It is not evidence that
the original position was secretly winning. Honest analysis distinguishes a saving chance
from a forced draw.

Defensive resources include perpetual-check possibilities, stalemate constructions,
promotion threats, fortresses, and tactical exchanges that eliminate the opponent's last
winning pawn. Which resource matters depends on the board. Random checks can help the
opponent escape to safety; purposeful checks may preserve a repeating route. Random
sacrifices can shorten the loss; purposeful sacrifices may remove the only winning
mechanism.

If you are winning, ask what the opponent is trying to make you forget. Their attack on a
rook may conceal a stalemate idea. Their apparently free pawn may open your king to checks.
Their last active piece may be worth exchanging even at the cost of a pawn. Conversion
improves when you understand the defender's hopes rather than dismissing them.

Exercise: take a successful sacrifice and write the best defense you can find, including
declining the offer and returning material. If the sacrifice still works, explain why. If it
fails, preserve the useful pattern but label the original move honestly. Learning from an
unsound combination is possible; learning that hope equals proof is costly.
