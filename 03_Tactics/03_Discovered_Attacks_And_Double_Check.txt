Discovered attacks and double check
===================================

A discovered attack occurs when one piece moves and reveals an attack by another piece
behind it. The moving piece may create an additional threat, so a single turn can produce
two problems. This is why a piece standing in front of a bishop, rook, or queen can be more
dangerous than it appears: it is a movable door on a line of attack.

The strongest discovered attacks give the moving piece freedom to choose a useful
destination. If revealing a rook attack on the queen forces the opponent to respond, the
moving knight may be able to capture a pawn, attack another piece, or improve its position
with tempo. The hidden line supplies the force; the moving piece supplies the extra benefit.

A discovered check reveals an attack on the king. Because check must be answered, the moving
piece can sometimes travel to a square that would otherwise be unsafe. That does not make
every such move sound. The opponent may capture the checking piece, block the check, or move
the king in a way that also solves the second threat. Calculate the actual responses.

Double check means that two pieces check the king at once. The checked king must move;
capturing or blocking only one checker does not solve the other check. This reduction in
defensive choices makes double check a powerful mating tool. The attacker still has to
account for every legal king escape. Two checks are not the same thing as checkmate.

Look for discovered attacks before exchanging the front piece. A knight that seems awkwardly
placed in front of your bishop may be storing tactical energy. Exchanging it immediately
might remove your best attacking possibility. Conversely, when the opponent has such a
battery, exchanging the rear long-range piece or changing the king's position can remove the
danger before the door opens.

Defensive awareness begins with asking what a move uncovers. When your opponent moves a
knight away from an apparently irrelevant square, trace the lines behind its starting point.
A newly opened bishop diagonal can matter more than the knight's destination. This habit
also catches discovered defenses: a retreat may reveal a rook that now protects a previously
loose piece.

Imagine a bishop aligned with the enemy king but blocked by its own knight. If the knight
can move with check of its own, both pieces attack the king. The important preconditions are
alignment, a movable blocker, and a useful destination. The position may also contain
defenders or escape squares that spoil the intended mate. The motif tells you where to
search, not what the result must be.

Practice by describing each move in two parts: what it does at its destination and what it
changes at its starting square. This is especially useful after pawn captures, because a
pawn can leave one file and open another diagonal simultaneously. Discoveries become easier
to see when you train yourself to notice vacancies as actively as occupied squares.
