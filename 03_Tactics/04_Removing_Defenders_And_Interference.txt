Removing defenders, deflection, and interference
================================================

An important target may be safe only because one defender performs a specific job. If that
defender can be exchanged, lured away, blocked, or overloaded, the target can become
vulnerable. Tactical combinations often look spectacular at the finish but begin with a
simple question: what is keeping this target safe?

Removing a defender is the broad idea. You may capture the defending piece directly or force
a trade that eliminates it. Deflection draws the defender away from its duty. Decoy draws a
piece onto a useful square, often into a fork, pin, or mating pattern. The terms overlap in
some combinations; understanding the mechanism matters more than choosing a perfect label.

Overloading occurs when one piece has more defensive duties than it can successfully
perform. A rook might guard both a back-rank mating square and a loose queen. If saving one
target abandons the other, the attacker may create a decisive choice. The defender is not
overloaded merely because it protects two things. The attacker must be able to exploit the
conflict in time.

Interference blocks a line between defender and target. A piece may be sacrificed on the
line to interrupt a rook's protection or a bishop's control. Sometimes the defender can
capture the interfering piece, but doing so changes its position or opens another line. The
full combination depends on those consequences, not simply on placing something between two
pieces.

Clearance is the related attacking idea of vacating a square or line so another piece can
use it. A rook might move with tempo to clear a diagonal for a bishop. A pawn sacrifice
might clear an entry square for a knight. Good calculation tracks the space being created as
carefully as the material being given away.

When considering a sacrifice, identify the defender's exact duty before calculating. 'This
bishop is important' is too vague. 'This bishop is the only piece preventing the queen from
entering next to the king' is specific enough to test. Then ask whether the opponent can
replace the defender, decline the sacrifice, trade queens, or create a stronger threat.

Defending against these ideas often means adding a second independent defender or separating
duties. If your rook is both shielding the king and protecting a pawn, a modest king escape
square may release the rook from the first duty. A queen trade may remove the mating threat
and make the overload disappear. Quiet coordination can neutralize a tactical problem before
a forcing combination exists.

Exercise: choose a vulnerable target in a game and list every actual defender. For each
defender, ask whether it can be exchanged, attacked with tempo, pinned, or interrupted. Then
perform the same examination for your own king and loose pieces. The exercise teaches that
tactics arise from relationships between pieces, not from isolated piece values.
