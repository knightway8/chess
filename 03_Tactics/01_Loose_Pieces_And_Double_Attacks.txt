Loose pieces, forks, and double attacks
=======================================

A loose piece is a piece that is undefended or insufficiently protected for the tactics in
the position. It does not have to be under attack already. Its vulnerability matters because
a future move can attack it while doing something else. When two targets can be attacked at
once, one defender may not have enough time to save both.

A fork is one familiar double attack: a single piece attacks two or more targets. Knights
are famous for forks because their attack pattern is easy to overlook, but every kind of
piece can create a double attack. A pawn can threaten two pieces; a queen can check the king
while attacking a rook; a king can attack two poorly placed minor pieces in an ending.

The value of a double attack depends on the targets and the replies. If both targets can
escape with a forcing move, the fork may win nothing. If one target is the king, the
opponent must address the check, which often makes the second target vulnerable. Even then,
verify that the forking piece cannot be captured and that the threatened material remains
available afterward.

Look for alignment before looking for a clever move. Are two valuable pieces within reach of
a knight? Do an exposed king and an undefended rook share a queen's possible checking line?
Can a pawn advance attack two pieces that lack good retreat squares? The tactical move often
becomes visible once you notice the geometric relationship between the targets.

Double attacks also arise through threats of different kinds. One move may threaten mate and
attack a bishop. Another may threaten promotion and win a pinned piece. The defender cannot
always compare these threats by material value alone. A mating threat generally outranks a
threat to win a rook; a promotion with check may interrupt an otherwise winning capture
sequence.

Defensively, do not respond to the first threat you see without identifying the second.
Sometimes you can meet both by exchanging the attacking piece, creating a stronger threat,
or moving one target so that it protects the other. Sometimes the best defense is to accept
a smaller material loss while preventing a larger one. Panic often turns a manageable fork
into a decisive loss.

Imagine your queen and an undefended bishop are both far from your king. An enemy queen
check may force your king to a particular square while also attacking the bishop along
another line. The mistake occurred before the check: you allowed an exposed king and a loose
piece to coexist without examining their relationship. This is why a quiet defensive move
can prevent tactics that have not yet appeared.

Training task: in each game you review, identify the loose pieces before every tactical
exchange. Do not label a piece safe merely because it has one defender; check whether that
defender is pinned, overloaded, or easily deflected. Over time, loose pieces become visual
warning signals, and many 'surprising' tactics stop being surprises.
