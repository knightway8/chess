The principle of two weaknesses
===============================

One weakness can often be defended. A king, rook, or knight can sit in front of a weak pawn
and hold it. A second weakness forces the defender to divide attention. The principle of two
weaknesses says that a lasting advantage is often converted by creating another target far
enough away that a single defensive setup cannot cover both.

The second weakness need not be a pawn. It can be an exposed king, a possible invasion, a
restricted piece, a threatened pawn break, or a passed pawn. What matters is that defending
it conflicts with the defense of the first problem. If both weaknesses are covered by the
same effortless move, you have not truly stretched the position.

Do not rush to attack the first target repeatedly. If every attacking piece is tied to one
pawn that the opponent can defend, you may have reached the limit of that plan. Switch
attention, improve your king, gain space on the other wing, or prepare a break. The existing
weakness can hold the defender in place while you create something elsewhere.

Distance matters because pieces take time to transfer. A rook may defend one wing but be
unable to stop a passer on the other. A knight is particularly sensitive to routes; forcing
it to choose between distant tasks can create a decisive tempo. Queens travel quickly and
may defend several fronts with checks, so queen exchanges can make the two-weakness method
easier when your king and rook are more active.

The defender should seek active counterplay before the second weakness settles. A pawn
sacrifice that activates a rook can be better than clinging to every weakness. Trading one
vulnerable pawn for an active enemy pawn may reduce the number of fronts. A fortress often
works because the defender has no independent weaknesses and can repeat a compact setup.

When creating a second front, preserve control. Advancing several pawns near your king may
create a target for the opponent instead of one for you. Prefer improvements that keep your
own structure sound and make the defender react. Your advantage should reduce risk, not
merely increase the number of moving parts.

Imagine an ending where the opponent's rook must guard a weak queenside pawn. If your king
can attack a kingside pawn, the rook may not be able to defend both. But if your king's
journey allows the opposing king to capture your own pawns, the plan fails. The principle
suggests a destination; calculation verifies the route.

Exercise: in a position where you have more space, name the first weakness, the defender
assigned to it, and a possible second target. Then name the opponent's best source of
counterplay. This final step prevents positional play from becoming one-sided storytelling.
