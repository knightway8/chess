Activity, initiative, and tempo
===============================

Activity measures how usefully pieces participate. An active piece attacks meaningful
targets, controls important routes, restricts the enemy, or supports a pawn break. A piece
is not active merely because it has moved far. A queen deep in enemy territory can be driven
around while the rest of its army remains undeveloped.

The initiative is the ability to pose threats that require responses. It is temporary unless
converted into material, structural gains, a lasting attack, or a favorable transition. When
you have the initiative, improve your position with tempo and prevent the opponent from
coordinating. When you face it, seek exchanges of attacking pieces, challenge the source of
the pressure, or return material to end the forcing sequence.

A tempo is one turn of play. Gaining a tempo means achieving something while forcing a
response; losing one means spending a turn that the position could not afford. Tempo is
contextual. Moving a developed piece twice can be wasteful in the opening, but essential if
the second move wins material or places the piece on a dominating square. A pawn move that
appears free can be a serious loss of time if the center opens before development is
complete.

Compare activity by examining jobs. Which rook has an open file? Which bishop has targets?
Which knight can reach a stable post? Which queen can enter without becoming a target? Count
useful options, not legal moves alone. A bishop may have many legal retreats and no
constructive task; a knight may have one excellent route that determines the position.

The fastest move is not always a forcing move. Suppose your opponent threatens to bring a
rook onto an open file. Racing to that file may create a sterile exchange. A pawn break
elsewhere might open a second front and make their rook placement irrelevant. Activity
should serve the position's main struggle, not imitate the opponent automatically.

When materially ahead, neutralizing activity often matters more than winning more pawns.
Trade the opponent's most active piece, block their passer, or give your king shelter before
collecting. When materially behind, avoid passive exchanges that leave no sources of
counterplay. Seek active pieces, asymmetry, and targets that require attention.

An initiative can be illusory if every threat is easily answered by a developing move. If
your queen attacks a pawn and the defender saves it while developing a knight, you may have
helped the opponent. Ask whether your threat makes the defender compromise. A threat that
improves the defender is rarely a great achievement.

Exercise: pause at a quiet middlegame and rank all four minor pieces by usefulness. Give a
concrete reason for each ranking. Then find one move that improves the worst piece without
creating a tactical defect. This simple discipline converts the vague advice to 'be active'
into a practical plan.
