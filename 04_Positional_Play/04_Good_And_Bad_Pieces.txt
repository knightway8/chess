Good pieces, bad pieces, and useful exchanges
=============================================

A good piece contributes to the current position; a bad piece lacks a useful role or is
restricted by the structure. These judgments are temporary. A blocked bishop can become
powerful after one pawn break, and a beautiful knight can become irrelevant after the play
shifts to the opposite wing.

A bishop restricted by its own pawns is often called bad. That description should lead to a
plan: move the pawns to another color, arrange the freeing break, exchange the bishop for an
active enemy piece, or give it a useful defensive role. Calling it bad without finding a
remedy is diagnosis without treatment.

A knight needs stable destinations. In a closed center it can maneuver around barriers that
stop bishops. In an open position with targets on both wings, it may struggle against a
bishop's range. Yet a centralized knight supported by a pawn can dominate even an open board
if the bishop cannot challenge it. General comparisons must yield to concrete placement.

The bishop pair often gains strength when the position is open or likely to open. One bishop
covers each color, and together they can influence both wings. Trading one bishop may be
correct to damage the enemy structure, eliminate a dangerous knight, win time, or survive an
attack. Preserve the pair for what it can do, not because a rule says two bishops deserve a
trophy.

Rooks need entry routes and targets. Doubling rooks on a file that leads nowhere may look
organized but accomplish little. A rook can be active behind a passed pawn, along an open
rank, or on a file where it can enter the enemy camp. Queens need safe access; an early
queen expedition may lose time to minor-piece attacks.

The exchange question should be phrased carefully: which of my pieces is least useful, which
enemy piece is most useful, and what changes after the trade? Trading your bad bishop for
their good knight can be excellent. It can also open a file against your king or create an
enemy passed pawn. Include the new structure in the answer.

Sometimes a piece is bad because it performs a necessary job. A passive rook may be the only
stopper of a passed pawn. Improving it without replacing that duty loses the game.
Coordination means that each necessary task remains covered as pieces change roles.

Exercise: assign every piece a current job in one of your middlegames. If a piece has no
job, give it a plausible route. If the route takes several turns, ask what the opponent can
do meanwhile. This turns piece improvement into a timed plan rather than a collection of
ideal squares.
