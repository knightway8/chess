Pawn storms, hooks, and opening lines
=====================================

A pawn storm advances pawns toward the opposing king to gain space and create contact with
its shelter. It is most common when the kings are on opposite wings, because advancing your
pawns does not directly expose your own king. With both kings on the same wing, a storm
demands more caution.

A hook is an enemy pawn that your pawn can attack to provoke an exchange and open a line. If
no hook exists, an advance may force the defender to create one or may gain space for
pieces. The attacking value comes from the lines created, not from how close the pawns
appear to the king.

Prepare the pieces that will use those lines. Opening a file is pointless if the opponent
controls it first. A rook should be ready, the queen should have a safe route, and minor
pieces should cover escape squares. If you push first and organize later, the defender may
close the line or strike in the center.

Speed matters in opposite-wing attacks. Count useful turns, not pawn distances alone. A move
that opens a file immediately may outrank an aesthetically pleasing advance. A defensive
move that forces the attacker to recapture can gain a critical tempo for the opposite
attack.

Do not capture automatically when the pawn fronts meet. Advancing past can keep lines
closed, while exchanging can open them. The correct choice depends on whose pieces are
prepared and which king is exposed. Sometimes the defender captures to trade attackers;
sometimes the attacker keeps tension to restrict defenders.

The defender can counter by closing the wing, exchanging attacking pieces, returning
material to eliminate a pawn, or opening the center. Attacking the storm's base may collapse
it. Advancing a shelter pawn can gain space but may also provide the hook the attacker
wanted.

A successful storm may transition into an ending rather than mate. If the attack wins a pawn
or damages structure, be ready to trade queens and convert. Refusing a favorable transition
because you expected checkmate can let the advantage disappear.

Exercise: in an opposite-wing game, count how many turns each side needed to make first
useful contact with the shelter. Include piece preparation. Then identify the move that
changed the race's tempo. This is more instructive than counting pawn pushes alone.
