Converting an initiative into something lasting
===============================================

An initiative is borrowed time. Your threats make the opponent respond, but if each response
improves their position and your threats achieve nothing, the initiative will fade.
Conversion means turning temporary activity into material, structural damage, a favorable
ending, or a continuing mating attack.

First identify the defender's consolidating move. They may want to complete development,
trade queens, close a file, or bring a knight to defense. Preventing that move can be more
important than making another superficial threat. A quiet restriction can preserve the
attack's energy.

Open another line only if your pieces can use it. Sacrificing a pawn to expose the king can
be sound when the defender cannot coordinate. Opening every line may also activate an enemy
rook and give the king an escape. The geometry after the exchange is the real object of
calculation.

Accept modest gains when they are durable. Winning a clean pawn and entering an active
ending may be better than continuing a speculative attack. Damaging the enemy pawn shelter
can remain valuable after queens are exchanged if the weaknesses become targets. Conversion
is not a failure of ambition; it is how advantages survive.

Keep enough pieces to maintain pressure, but exchange key defenders. A blanket rule against
exchanges during an attack is too crude. Trading the defender that controls the only entry
square may decide the game. Trading your best attacker for a passive piece may end the
attack.

If the attack runs out, stop sacrificing. Reassess material and king safety, activate the
worst piece, and protect new weaknesses. Many games are lost because a player feels
obligated to continue an attack whose objective basis has vanished.

The defender seeks a moment when an exchange or pawn break changes the rhythm. If you are
attacking, calculate those moments first. A move that works against a waiting defense may
fail to a central counterstrike with tempo.

Exercise: find the moment in an attacking win where the advantage became permanent. Was it
mate, material, a ruined structure, or a dominant ending? Then find the last moment the
defender could have consolidated. These two moments explain the attack better than the final
combination alone.
