Pawn majorities, minority attacks, and races
============================================

A pawn majority means having more pawns than the opponent on a section of the board. A
healthy majority can sometimes create a passed pawn through exchanges. It is potential, not
a guarantee. Doubled pawns, a blockade, or an active enemy king can prevent the extra pawn
from producing anything.

To use a majority, advance the pawn whose movement preserves flexibility and support. Avoid
creating a backward pawn without reason. Calculate how exchanges change the count on
adjacent files. The goal is usually to force a passer while keeping the opponent from
blockading the whole formation.

A minority attack advances fewer pawns against a larger enemy pawn group to provoke a fixed
weakness. This sounds paradoxical because the attacker is outnumbered locally. The purpose
is structural: exchanges can leave a backward or isolated pawn that becomes a target for
pieces. A minority attack is not automatically a king attack, even though it uses wing
pawns.

Timing matters. If the center is unstable or your king is vulnerable, a slow minority
advance may be irrelevant. The opponent may use the space to launch a faster attack or push
past to gain territory. Prepare piece placement so that the weakness created by the pawn
contact can be attacked immediately.

Pawn races require exact counting. Count turns to promotion, whose turn it is, whether
promotion gives check, and whether a new queen can stop the other pawn. Include king
captures and diagonal pawn captures that alter the route. A pawn apparently one step behind
can win if it promotes with tempo.

The rule of the square is a visual method for deciding whether a bare king can catch a lone
pawn without interference. It is useful, but blocked paths, opposing kings, checks, and
other pawns can change the result. Apply the method only to the simplified circumstances it
assumes.

When both wings contain majorities, choose whether to create a passer, hold the opponent's
majority, or use one side as a distraction. The king's position may decide which majority
matters. A distant majority can be valuable because it stretches the defender, even when it
advances slowly.

Exercise: set up a pawn race in a legal board tool and predict the result without moving the
pieces. Then verify it. Repeat after changing whose turn it is and after giving one pawn a
checking promotion. Small changes teach why exact tempo counting outranks intuition.
