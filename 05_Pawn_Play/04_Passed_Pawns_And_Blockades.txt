Passed pawns and blockades
==========================

A passed pawn has no opposing pawn on its file or either neighboring file capable of
stopping it through ordinary pawn play. It may still be blocked or captured by pieces. Its
power comes from the obligation it can impose: an enemy piece may have to watch it while the
rest of your army acts elsewhere.

An outside passed pawn is far from the main pawn mass. In an ending, it can draw the
defending king away and let your king invade the other wing. A protected passed pawn is
guarded by another pawn and can be difficult to blockade. Connected passed pawns support
each other and become increasingly dangerous as they advance.

Advance a passer when the advance improves its prospects or gains time. Pushing blindly can
move it onto a square where it is blockaded and attacked. Sometimes the pawn's greatest
value is as a restraint: its mere ability to advance ties down a rook. Improve your king and
pieces before revealing the pawn's final route.

The square in front of a passed pawn is often an excellent blockading post. A knight can be
strong there because pawns cannot drive it away easily and it can attack around the
blockade. A king is the ideal blockader in many endings if it can arrive safely. Heavy
pieces may blockade reluctantly because passive duty wastes their range.

Rooks usually belong behind passed pawns, whether the pawn is friendly or hostile, because
they remain active as the pawn advances. This is a useful guide, not an absolute rule. A
rook may need to check from the side, attack another weakness, or occupy an active rank.
Calculate checks and king shelter before obeying placement slogans.

Passed pawns can be tactical weapons. A sacrifice may clear their path, promotion may come
with check, and one passer can deflect a defender from another. Count moves accurately and
account for forcing checks. A pawn race is not settled by visual distance alone.

When stopping a passer, consider blockade, attack from behind, king approach, and
counterplay with your own passer. Giving up a piece may be correct if the remaining ending
is drawn. Giving it up too early may remove the piece that could have stopped a second pawn.

Exercise: in every pawn ending, identify candidate passers before calculating king routes.
Then ask which passer is a real promotion threat and which mainly serves as a decoy. The
distinction shapes where the kings belong.
