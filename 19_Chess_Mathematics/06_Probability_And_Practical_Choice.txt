Probability, risk, and practical choice
=======================================

Chess has no dice in ordinary play, but human decisions involve uncertainty. You may be
unsure whether a sacrifice is sound, whether the opponent knows an opening trap, or whether
you can execute a difficult ending with little time. Probability can organize these
uncertainties without pretending to prove the position.

Expected score combines outcomes with probabilities. If a risky choice gives an estimated
fifty-percent chance to win, ten-percent chance to draw, and forty-percent chance to lose,
its expected tournament score is one-half times 0.50 plus one-half of the draw probability,
giving 0.55. The calculation is only as reliable as the estimates.

Objective evaluation and practical choice can differ. In a must-win final round, a complex
position with greater variance may be preferable to a sterile draw. When a draw secures
first place, reducing counterplay may have extra practical value. This does not change which
moves are best under perfect play; it changes the utility attached to possible results.

Do not confuse frequency with correctness. If an opening scores sixty percent in a small
database, player strength, color imbalance, selection effects, and repeated games can
distort the statistic. A line popular among stronger players may look better because of who
plays it. Large samples reduce random noise but do not remove bias.

Conditional probability matters. The chance of a tactic working against an opponent who has
already spent time and recognized the theme differs from its overall online success rate.
Update your practical judgment from relevant evidence, while continuing to calculate the
position.

Risk management means avoiding outcomes you cannot afford while preserving winning chances.
A move that is slightly less precise but easy to execute can be practical in time pressure.
This idea should not excuse a known tactical error. A move that loses by force has low
practical merit against any opponent who can find the refutation.

Use probabilities humbly. State a range instead of fake precision, identify what could
change your estimate, and review afterward. The board supplies facts; probability describes
your uncertainty about them.

Exercise: for one difficult decision, estimate win, draw, and loss chances for two
candidates, ensuring each set totals one hundred percent. Then analyze the position and note
which assumption changed most.
