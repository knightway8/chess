How many moves are possible at the start?
=========================================

In the initial position, the player moving first has exactly twenty legal choices. Sixteen
are pawn advances: each of the eight pawns can move either one step or two. Four are knight
moves: each of the two knights has two destinations. Every other piece is blocked.

After any one of those twenty choices, the second player also has twenty legal choices. That
gives four hundred legal move sequences after one turn by each side. A sequence is a path
through play. Two different sequences can sometimes reach the same position later, so path
count and position count are different quantities.

Continuing exhaustive legal enumeration from the initial position gives 8,902 paths after
three individual turns, 197,281 after four, 4,865,609 after five, and 119,060,324 after six.
Computer-chess programmers call this kind of move-tree count a performance test, usually
shortened to perft. It is used to test whether a move generator handles checks, captures,
castling rights, promotions, and the passing pawn capture correctly.

These are exact leaf counts for the stated depth, not estimates of how many 'good moves'
exist. They also do not count the internal nodes above the leaves. At depth four, for
example, the figure 197,281 counts complete four-turn paths. If someone counts every
intermediate position visited while building the tree, they will report a different total.

The effective branching factor changes by level. Dividing 8,902 by 400 gives about 22.26
continuations per path between the second and third turns. Dividing 197,281 by 8,902 gives
about 22.16 at the next level. Dividing 4,865,609 by 197,281 gives about 24.66. The game
does not have a fixed number of choices per turn. Checks restrict choices; open positions
and promotions can increase them.

The growth is multiplicative. If a hypothetical game offered exactly twenty choices on every
turn, five turns would yield twenty multiplied by itself five times, or 3,200,000 paths.
Real chess gives 4,865,609 from the start at that depth because the number of replies varies
and grows as pieces become mobile.

Do not say 'there are 119,060,324 positions after three moves' without qualification. Chess
players often use the word move for a turn by both players, while programmers often measure
depth in single-player turns called plies. The exact number above applies to six plies, and
it counts paths. It can include repeated destination positions reached through different
orders.

Source note: the figures in this lesson match the published initial-position results at
https://chessprogramming.org/Perft_Results. They can also be reproduced by a correct legal
move generator. The distinction between paths, nodes, and unique positions is part of the
result, not technical trivia.
