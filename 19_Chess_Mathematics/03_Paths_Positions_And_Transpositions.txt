Paths, positions, and transpositions
====================================

A path is a sequence of moves. A position is the complete game state after a path. Different
paths can lead to the same position; this is a transposition. Counting paths therefore
overcounts unique positions whenever move order can change without changing the final state.

The visible arrangement is not always the complete state. Whose turn it is matters. Castling
rights matter even when king and rook appear on their original squares, because they may
have moved away and returned. Eligibility for the special passing pawn capture can matter
for one turn. The progress counter used by draw rules also matters for game administration.
Two boards that look identical can represent different legal states.

This distinction explains opening transpositions. A player may begin with a flank move, then
later create a pawn structure normally associated with a queen-pawn opening. The same
strategic position can arrive through several named routes. Opening names are classification
tools, not separate universes.

Engines use transposition tables to store information about positions already searched. If a
different move order reaches the same encoded state, the program can reuse prior work. The
table must preserve the state information relevant to legal moves and evaluation. Treating
visual piece placement alone as identity could introduce errors.

Repetition rules use a precise idea of the same position. The same player must have the
move, pieces must occupy the same squares, and possible moves must be the same; changes in
castling rights or passing-capture availability can make positions different. Competition
claim procedures add another layer, so players should consult the current official rules.

For learners, the practical lesson is to recognize structures across openings. If two move
orders reach the same pawn structure and piece placement, the plans may transfer. If one
route has forced a concession or removed a right, the positions may only look similar.

A database statistic can also count games, positions, or occurrences. One million games are
not one million unique positions. A popular position may occur in thousands of games. Always
ask what the unit of a large chess number is.

Exercise: describe two opening routes that could develop different pieces first and later
converge. Then list the hidden state conditions that must also match before calling the
final positions identical.
