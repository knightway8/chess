Tempo, distance, and race calculations
======================================

Chess distance is measured in legal turns, not physical centimeters. A rook may cross an
open board in one turn, a knight may need several jumps, and a king can move diagonally to
approach two goals at once. Obstacles and controlled squares change every route.

A tempo is one turn. If a pawn needs four advances to promote and another needs three, the
shorter route seems faster. Now add whose turn it is, captures, checking promotions, and
queen checks. The apparent one-tempo advantage may disappear or become decisive.

Use a timeline. Write the first side's intended action, then the second side's best reply,
alternating until promotion or capture. Never count one side's moves in isolation. If
promotion gives check, place that forced response on the timeline before continuing the
other race.

King routes use Chebyshev distance on an empty board: because a king can move diagonally,
the minimum number of steps between two squares equals the larger of the horizontal and
vertical separations. This mathematical fact is only a starting point. Enemy control,
occupied squares, opposition, and check can make the shortest geometric path illegal.

Knight distance behaves differently because of its jump. A knight close by straight-line
distance may not reach a target immediately, while a seemingly indirect move may be fastest.
Learn routes by practicing exact positions rather than trying to convert visual distance
into a simple formula over the board.

Tempo also appears in development. If one side spends three turns moving the queen while the
other develops three pieces with attacks on it, the second side has gained useful activity.
The arithmetic matters only if the moves are useful and the position can open. A lead
measured as 'two tempi' does not automatically convert to material.

Reserve tempi are moves saved to alter whose turn it is. In pawn endings, one spare advance
can force the opposing king to yield. Count these reserves before fixing the structure.

Exercise: create a verbal race with two passers, then list every assumption needed to
predict it. Verify on a legal board. Add a checking promotion and observe how one forcing
tempo changes the result.
