Counting the board without inventing a number
=============================================

The chessboard has sixty-four squares because it contains eight rows and eight columns. It
begins with thirty-two pieces, so exactly half the squares are occupied. Each side begins
with sixteen pieces: one king, one queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights, and eight
pawns.

These small exact counts do not make position counting simple. If you merely choose
thirty-two occupied squares from sixty-four, the binomial coefficient is
1,832,624,140,942,590,534. That counts only choices of occupied locations. It does not
assign piece types or colors, track whose turn it is, enforce one king per side, or
eliminate illegal arrangements. It is neither the number of chess positions nor a useful
estimate by itself.

Multiplying arbitrary choices can easily produce nonsense. You cannot place every piece
independently on any square because two pieces cannot occupy one square. Pawns face
direction and cannot legally remain on their promotion row. Kings cannot occupy mutually
illegal locations. Some arrangements cannot arise from the initial material because
promotions and captures impose historical constraints.

A legal-position count must define 'legal.' Does it mean reachable from the initial setup by
some legal sequence? Does it include positions that could exist only after many promotions?
Does it distinguish draw counters and castling rights? Does it count visually identical
boards separately when the player to move differs? Different definitions answer different
questions.

Combinatorics remains useful when the sample space is defined. There are sixty-four choices
for the location of one hypothetical marker. There are sixty-four times sixty-three ordered
choices for two distinguishable markers without overlap, or 4,032. If the markers are
indistinguishable, divide by two and obtain 2,016 unordered pairs. The difference between
distinguishable and indistinguishable objects appears throughout chess counting.

Do not trust a huge number merely because it contains factorials and powers. Check whether
choices are independent, whether order matters, whether illegal cases were removed, and
whether histories create distinct states. A transparent lower or upper bound can be more
informative than an unexplained 'exact' number.

Exercise: invent a simplified board with only two kings and one rook. List the constraints a
position counter would need before attempting arithmetic. Defining the object correctly is
the first mathematical move.
