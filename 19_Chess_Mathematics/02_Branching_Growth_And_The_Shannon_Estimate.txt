Branching growth and the famous huge estimate
=============================================

A game tree contains choices followed by replies followed by new choices. If the average
number of legal continuations is b and the search looks d individual turns ahead, a simple
uniform model has about b raised to the power d leaf paths. This is a model, because real
branches have different lengths and different numbers of moves.

The exponent explains the explosion. With an average of thirty choices per individual turn,
looking four turns ahead produces about 810,000 leaves. Six turns produces about 729
million. Eight turns produces about 656.1 billion. Increasing depth by one multiplies the
leaf count by about thirty in this simplified model.

Claude Shannon's 1950 paper on programming a computer for chess used an order-of-magnitude
argument. He described roughly a thousand choices for a complete move by both players and a
typical game of about forty such complete moves. Multiplying a thousand by itself forty
times gives ten raised to the power 120. This quantity became known informally as the
Shannon number.

Ten raised to the power 120 is a one followed by 120 zeros. It is a heuristic estimate of
game-tree complexity, not an exact count of all legal games, not an exact count of legal
positions, and not a proof that every game lasts forty moves with a thousand choices each
time. Different assumptions produce different estimates. The value is useful because it
communicates scale.

Why does a thousand per complete move roughly correspond to thirty per individual turn?
Thirty choices for one side followed by about thirty replies gives nine hundred two-turn
paths. Rounding that to an order of a thousand makes the forty-move estimate convenient.
Using thirty choices across eighty individual turns gives about 1.48 times ten raised to the
power 118, within a few orders of magnitude of the famous estimate.

Search programs survive by avoiding uniform exhaustive search. They order promising moves,
cut off branches that cannot affect the decision, reuse evaluations of repeated positions,
and extend critical lines selectively. Human calculation also prunes. A player investigates
forcing replies and rejects a candidate after finding one decisive refutation.

Pruning carries a lesson and a risk. It saves enormous effort, but a move excluded too early
can be the only defense. Strong calculation combines selective attention with tactical
discipline. Checks, captures, promotions, and threats receive priority because their
consequences constrain the tree.

Source note: Shannon's paper, 'Programming a Computer for Playing Chess,' appeared in 1950
and is available as a scan at
https://walkofmind.com/programming/chess/Shannon%20-%20Programming%20a%20computer%20for%20playing%20chess.pdf.
The large number here is presented with his modeling assumptions so it is not mistaken for
an exact census.
