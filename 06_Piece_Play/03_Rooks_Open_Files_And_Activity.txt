Rooks, open files, and active ranks
===================================

Rooks are long-range pieces that usually need files and ranks cleared of friendly pawns.
They often begin the game waiting for the structure to open. Development therefore means
more than moving each rook once; it means connecting them, choosing useful lines, and
preventing them from being trapped in passive defense.

An open file contains no pawns. A half-open file contains no pawn of one side but may
contain an opposing pawn. Occupying such a file is useful when it offers an entry point,
attacks a weakness, or restricts enemy pieces. A rook on a file with no destination may be
less active than a rook behind a passed pawn.

Entry squares decide file control. Before doubling rooks, ask where the front rook can enter
and whether that square is safe. If every entrance is guarded, use a pawn break, exchange
the guarding piece, or switch files. Doubling for appearance alone can spend two turns
without creating a threat.

Rooks become powerful on active ranks inside enemy territory because they can attack pawns
from the side and restrict the king. Getting there safely often matters more than winning a
pawn immediately. Once the rook penetrates, preserve its escape routes and watch for traps
by the enemy king and minor pieces.

In endings, activity frequently outweighs a pawn. A rook tied to passive defense may be
unable to check the enemy king or attack pawns. Sacrificing a pawn to activate the rook can
improve drawing or winning chances. This decision requires calculation, since some pawn
deficits are simply decisive.

Connected rooks defend each other and can double on a line. Keep them coordinated, but do
not reject a useful split if each rook has a clear job. One may support a passer while the
other gives checks. Rigid rules about keeping rooks together can miss the position's
demands.

Rooks generally work well behind passed pawns because the line remains useful as the pawn
moves. Side checks, frontal blockades, and attacks on the pawn's base can be better in
specific endings. The king's shelter and checking distance often determine the correct
placement.

Exercise: for each rook in a middlegame, name its route, entry point, and target. If the
file has no entry point, name the structural change required to create one. This prevents
the common mistake of confusing an open file with an accomplished invasion.
