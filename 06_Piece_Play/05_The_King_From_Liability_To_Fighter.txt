The king: shelter first, activity later
=======================================

In the opening and middlegame, king safety often limits every plan. Open central lines,
missing shelter pawns, and enemy heavy pieces can turn a small inaccuracy into mate. In an
ending with fewer attacking pieces, the king becomes a powerful short-range fighter.
Recognizing the transition is a central chess skill.

Castling usually helps by moving the king away from the center and connecting a rook. It is
not a guarantee of safety. If the shelter pawns have advanced and the opponent's pieces
already point toward that wing, castling there can place the king in the attack. Sometimes
leaving the king temporarily central is safer while the center remains closed.

King safety is relational. Count attackers, defenders, open lines, and possible pawn breaks.
A king with no nearby pawns may be safe if queens are gone and the enemy pieces lack access.
A king behind three pawns may be unsafe if a sacrifice can open lines and the defenders are
far away.

Create escape squares thoughtfully. A shelter pawn advance can prevent back-rank mate while
weakening a diagonal or providing an attacking hook. If there is no immediate danger, choose
the pawn move least likely to create a useful target. If danger is immediate, seek a forcing
defense instead of assuming an escape square can be made in time.

In endings, centralize the king when checks and mating nets no longer make that dangerous.
The king can attack and defend pawns, support promotion, blockade passers, and restrict its
counterpart. A king one move closer can change a drawn pawn ending into a win.

Opposition describes king placement where one king can force the other to yield access
because kings cannot stand adjacent. Distant and diagonal forms extend the idea, but spare
pawn moves and side routes often decide who benefits. Learn the geometry through legal
positions and exact verification rather than treating 'take the opposition' as a magic
instruction.

Do not activate the king into a tactic. A rook or bishop may control its route, and a queen
can exploit exposed placement with checks. Even in an ending, recalculate after every
exchange because the remaining piece may still create mating threats.

Exercise: identify the moment in one game when the king should have changed from protected
object to active piece. Was the queen trade enough, or did rooks and passers still create
danger? This teaches the transition instead of reducing king activity to a phase label.
