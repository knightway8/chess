Queens, power, and exposure
===========================

The queen combines rook-like and bishop-like movement, giving it unmatched range. That
strength makes every attack on the queen a possible gain of time. Early queen movement can
be justified by a concrete tactic, but casual expeditions often help the opponent develop
while the queen retreats.

A queen is most effective when other pieces support its entry. Alone near the enemy king, it
may give checks without a mating net and can become trapped. Coordinated with a bishop,
knight, or rook, it can exploit two kinds of line at once. Count controlled escape squares
and defenders rather than assuming queen proximity equals an attack.

Queen trades change the character of a position. They often reduce direct mating danger,
increase the relative importance of kings, and make structural weaknesses easier to attack.
A queen exchange can favor the side with an extra pawn, but not if the resulting pawn ending
loses or the opponent's king becomes overwhelmingly active.

When offered a queen trade, compare the position after the recapture. Which piece
recaptures, where does it land, which file opens, and whose king improves? Declining can
preserve attacking chances or avoid structural damage. Accepting can end counterplay. The
exchange itself has no moral value.

Queens are strong at creating simultaneous threats. A check can also attack a loose piece,
and a queen may switch wings in one move. Defending against a queen often requires
coordination rather than chasing it with scattered pawns. Each pawn advance can create new
entry squares.

In queen endings, perpetual checks are a constant resource. King shelter, checking distance,
and support for passed pawns matter enormously. Advancing a passer without securing the king
can expose you to endless checks. A queen may need to shield first and promote later.

Do not count a queen as three minor pieces in every position simply because common values
are close. Three coordinated pieces can cover many squares and create a mating net, while a
queen can attack loose targets and give repeated checks. Evaluate king safety, coordination,
and pawns alongside material.

Exercise: review each queen move in one game. State whether it created a threat, improved
coordination, answered danger, or merely moved the queen. Then count how many developing
moves the opponent gained by attacking it. This reveals whether queen activity was real or
expensive motion.
