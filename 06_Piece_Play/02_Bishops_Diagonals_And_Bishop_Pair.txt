Bishops, diagonals, and the bishop pair
=======================================

A bishop travels along one color for the entire game. Its strength depends on open
diagonals, targets on its color, and whether play occurs on one wing or both. A bishop
behind a central pawn chain may be restricted now yet powerful after the correct pawn break.

A good bishop operates outside its own pawn chain or has active targets. A bad bishop is
obstructed by friendly pawns, especially when those pawns are fixed. The label is relative.
A supposedly bad bishop can defend the pawns that restrict it, support a freeing advance, or
later emerge after exchanges.

The bishop pair controls both colors and often becomes stronger as lines open. Its long
range is valuable when there are targets on both wings. The opponent may seek a blockade, a
stable knight outpost, or an exchange of one bishop before opening the position. Keeping two
bishops is useful when the future position gives them work.

An opposite-colored bishop middlegame can favor the attacker because the defending bishop
cannot contest the attacker's color. With queens and rooks removed, opposite-colored bishops
often increase drawing chances because each bishop protects squares the other cannot
challenge. The change in supporting pieces changes the meaning of the same bishop relation.

A fianchetto places a bishop on a long diagonal behind a one-step knight-pawn advance. The
bishop can pressure the center and provide king shelter, but exchanging it may weaken
squares around the king. Do not preserve it blindly; trading it can win material, remove an
attacker, or obtain a favorable ending.

To improve a bishop, open its diagonal with a pawn move, relocate it to another diagonal,
exchange the obstructing pawn, or trade the bishop for a more influential enemy piece. Check
whether opening the diagonal also exposes your king. A line benefits every piece that can
use it, including the opponent's queen.

Bishops can dominate knights by controlling their routes from a distance. Knights can
dominate bishops when pawns lock the bishop behind its own structure and provide stable
posts. Ask which structure is likely after the next break. Piece comparisons become most
useful when tied to a predicted transformation.

Exercise: for each bishop in one game, identify its best diagonal, its current blocker, and
the pawn break that changes its life. Then decide whether the break helps your opponent
more. This turns abstract bishop appreciation into a concrete structural plan.
