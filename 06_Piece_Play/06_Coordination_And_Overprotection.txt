Coordination, redundancy, and overprotection
============================================

Pieces are coordinated when their tasks support one another. A bishop protects a rook's
entry square, a queen supports a pawn break, and a knight covers the king while attacking a
central target. Coordinated pieces can switch roles without leaving the position unguarded.

Simply defending the same pawn many times is not always coordination. Redundant defenders
are useful when they gain freedom after an exchange or when the pawn is central to the whole
position. They are wasteful when every piece becomes passive around a target the opponent
cannot attack.

Overprotection is the deliberate extra support of an important point, often a central pawn
or outpost. The supporting pieces may become well placed and ready for action elsewhere. The
concept works when the protected point organizes the position. It should not be used to
justify piling pieces behind a weakness without a plan.

Loose coordination often appears after one piece chases material. The queen captures a
remote pawn, the rook loses protection, and the king's defenders become overloaded. Before
accepting material, check whether the capturing piece was connecting parts of your army or
guarding a critical route.

Coordination has a time dimension. Two pieces may point at the same target, but if one can
be exchanged before the other arrives, the attack is not coordinated enough. Prepare threats
so defenders cannot solve them one at a time. Likewise, a defense should challenge the
attacker before all its pieces assemble.

When one piece performs several essential jobs, your position may be brittle. Add a
defender, move the king, or simplify so those duties are separated. This reduces tactical
overload. A move that appears to 'do nothing' can be valuable if it releases a rook or queen
from permanent defensive work.

Good coordination does not require symmetry. One rook can be active while another guards the
back rank. A knight can block a passer while a bishop attacks the other wing. The question
is whether the combined jobs address the position's needs and whether any necessary task is
forgotten.

Exercise: draw a verbal network for one position. For each piece, name what it protects,
attacks, and enables. Find the piece with too many duties and the piece with no useful duty.
A strong improving move often redistributes that workload.
