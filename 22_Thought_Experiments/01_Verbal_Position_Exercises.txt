Verbal thought experiments
==========================

These are relationship exercises, not exact tactical puzzles. No complete board is
specified, so no forced line is claimed.

ONE: You have an extra pawn, active queen, and exposed king. The opponent's queen can give
repeated checks. Compare taking another pawn with offering a queen trade. What facts about
the pawn ending must you know before choosing?

TWO: Your knight occupies a protected central outpost but attacks no target. The opponent
can open a rook file on the other wing. Decide whether to preserve the knight, reroute it,
or create counterplay. What makes an outpost relevant?

THREE: Your opponent has one weak pawn defended by every piece. You cannot add another
attacker. Identify three ways to create a second weakness and the counterplay each might
permit.

FOUR: A bishop is blocked by your own central pawns. One pawn break frees it but opens a
line toward your king. List preparatory moves that could make the break safe.

FIVE: You can win a rook with a fork, but the forking move abandons defense of mate. Which
part of your decision process should catch the error?

SIX: You lead in development and can sacrifice a pawn to open the center. Name the evidence
that would justify the sacrifice and the defender's best type of response.

SEVEN: A rook ending offers a choice between winning a pawn passively and keeping the rook
active. Describe how you would compare them without relying only on material.

EIGHT: Your opponent makes an unusual opening move that does not fight for the center.
Construct a principled response and explain why trying to punish it immediately could fail.

NINE: You can trade your bad bishop for the opponent's strong knight, but the recapture
creates a passed pawn. List the resulting features before deciding.

TEN: Both sides are racing passers. Your pawn promotes second but with check. Create a
timeline and explain why the checking tempo could reverse the visual race.
