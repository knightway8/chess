Bishop and knight endings
=========================

Bishops favor open play, targets on both wings, and pawns fixed on squares they can attack.
Knights favor stable posts, closed structures, and compact play. These tendencies become
stronger when few other pieces remain.

In same-colored bishop endings, a weak pawn on the bishop's color can be a lasting target.
In opposite-colored bishop endings, the defender may build a blockade on the color the
attacking bishop cannot control. Extra pawns do not guarantee a win.

Knight endings often resemble pawn endings because knights cannot give distant checks
easily. King activity and passed-pawn races are critical. A knight may be slow to change
wings, so an outside passer can be powerful.

A bishop can sometimes sacrifice itself for the last pawns on its color. A knight may stop
connected pawns through a fork or blockade. Count exact routes and remember that a knight
requires several moves to cross the board.

Exchange decisions should reflect the remaining pawn sides. A bishop can handle both wings
quickly; a knight may dominate one wing but arrive late at the other. A fixed central
outpost can reverse that range advantage.

Exercise: identify the best pawn target and the best king entry route before moving the
minor piece. The piece should support those goals instead of wandering toward a visually
central square.
