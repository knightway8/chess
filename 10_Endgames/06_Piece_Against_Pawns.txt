A piece against pawns
=====================

A bishop or knight may have to stop several pawns. The result depends on pawn connection,
advancement, king support, and the piece's geometry. A simple material count cannot settle
it.

A bishop can stop pawns from a distance when they advance on squares it controls. Pawns on
the opposite color can evade its line, though the king may supply the missing control. A
knight can blockade and fork pawns but may be overloaded by passers on distant wings.

Connected passers become dangerous when they protect each other. Blockade before they
advance far enough to create promotion threats with tempo. Separate passers can stretch a
knight, while a bishop may switch wings quickly if diagonals remain open.

The side with the piece should activate the king. A piece alone may hold the pawns without
winning them, and a careless sacrifice can leave a lost pawn ending. The side with pawns
should use the king to attack the blockader and avoid advancing into an easy fork.

Promotion with check changes races. Underpromotion can create a necessary fork or avoid
stalemate. Calculate legal promotion choices rather than assuming a queen ends the analysis.

Exercise: identify which pawn must be stopped first and which can be allowed to advance.
Defensive priority is often more important than attacking the nearest pawn.
