When to exchange and what survives
==================================

An exchange changes material, lines, pawn structure, king safety, and piece roles. Judge the
resulting position rather than the act of trading. An equal exchange can be positionally
decisive if it removes the only defender of a weak color or enters a winning pawn ending.

When ahead, trade the opponent's sources of counterplay. That often means exchanging queens
or an active piece, but a pawn trade that creates a dangerous enemy passer can be wrong.
When behind, preserve active possibilities rather than material for its own sake. These are
priorities, not automatic commands.

Ask five questions: what recaptures, which line opens, which pawn changes, whose king
improves, and what plan remains? Recapture choice can matter as much as the initial trade. A
queen recapture may centralize the queen but expose it to tempo; a pawn recapture may
strengthen the center but damage structure.

Trade good enemy pieces for less useful pieces when the structural cost is acceptable.
Preserve your active piece if the opponent's bad piece has no future. A currently bad bishop
can become good after a planned break, so include likely transformations.

Simplification means reducing complexity, not merely reducing material. A queen exchange can
lead to a sharp pawn race. Keeping queens can sometimes freeze both sides through
perpetual-check danger. Describe the new decisions before calling a line simple.

Exercise: before three exchanges in one game, predict the surviving best piece, weakest
pawn, and safest king. Compare your prediction with what happened. This creates a practical
sense for transitions.
