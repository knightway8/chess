Piece values and real value
===========================

The usual beginner scale assigns a pawn about one unit, a knight or bishop about three, a
rook about five, and a queen about nine. Different teaching systems and evaluation methods
use different numbers. Treat the scale as a convenient comparison tool, not a physical law.
The king has no exchange price because losing the king's safety can end the game.

Material counting answers a narrow question: what resources remain if the activity of those
resources is roughly comparable? That qualification is enormous. A trapped rook is not doing
a rook's job. A bishop dominating a long diagonal can be more useful than a rook with no
entry route. Two coordinated pieces can cover more independent targets than one queen, while
the queen may exploit checks that the pieces cannot prevent.

Start every exchange calculation with the whole sequence. If your bishop captures a rook and
is then captured by a pawn, you have usually won the exchange: you traded a minor piece for
a rook. If your queen captures a defended pawn and is trapped afterward, the first capture
did not win a pawn in any meaningful sense. Count everything that can be forced, including
intermediate checks and promotions.

After the count, ask what the surviving pieces can do. A trade that leaves you with the only
bishop capable of attacking fixed weak pawns may be excellent. A trade that leaves your
remaining bishop blocked by your own pawns may be disappointing even when the arithmetic is
equal. The quality of the pieces that remain often matters more than the attractiveness of
the capture itself.

Pawn value changes with structure and time. A healthy extra pawn can become a passed pawn
after exchanges. A doubled pawn may help control an important central square or open a
useful file for a rook. A remote passer can tie down the enemy king even when it never
promotes. A pawn close to promotion may demand a whole rook to stop it. These are positional
roles, not contradictions of the counting scale.

Compensation means receiving something useful for material: activity, king exposure, a
durable outpost, a powerful passer, or a long-term bind. Compensation must be identified,
not merely asserted. If you sacrifice a pawn for development, ask which pieces gained useful
time and what threats they can make before the opponent catches up. A vague feeling of
initiative is not a receipt for your investment.

Consider two choices while ahead a bishop. One wins another pawn but lets the opponent's
queen enter with checks. The other returns a pawn, trades queens, and leaves a
straightforward extra-piece ending. The second often makes the material advantage more real.
Winning more material is not always the best way to use material already won.

Exercise: for three exchanges in a recent game, write two verdicts. First give the material
balance. Then describe king safety, activity, pawn weaknesses, and the best surviving piece.
If the two verdicts disagree, investigate why. This is the beginning of evaluating chess
rather than merely keeping score.
