Board geometry without coordinates
==================================

The board has eight rows and eight columns, with alternating light and dark squares. You can
understand much of its geometry through relationships: adjacent, opposite, behind, in front,
on the same diagonal, and separated by a file. These descriptions are enough for this
library's conceptual lessons. Exact tactical positions require exact piece placement, so the
examples here teach relationships rather than claiming a forced sequence from an unspecified
board.

A file is a column running between the players. A rank is a row running from side to side. A
diagonal connects squares of the same color. The kingside is the half where the kings begin;
the queenside is the half where the queens begin. These names stay fixed when the kings
move. The center matters because central pieces can often reach either wing quickly, and
central pawns influence the routes available to those pieces.

Long-range pieces need lines. A bishop needs diagonals, a rook needs files or ranks, and a
queen can use both kinds of route. One pawn can block several lines at once. Removing that
pawn can change the board much more than its modest material value suggests. Before any pawn
exchange, look beyond the captured pawn and ask which bishop, rook, or queen will suddenly
see farther.

Knights need destinations and routes. They can jump over pieces, but that does not make
every journey short. A knight can be close to a target in ordinary distance yet need several
moves to reach a useful attacking square. A central knight often has more options than a
corner knight. Nevertheless, an edge square can be correct if it wins material, blocks a
passer, or leads to an excellent outpost. Geometry creates possibilities; the position
decides their value.

A king can travel diagonally as well as straight. This makes some diagonal routes no slower
than apparently direct routes. A king may approach an enemy pawn while also moving toward
its own passer. The useful idea is dual purpose: choose a route that preserves more than one
possibility. Do not count distance by how far apart two objects look. Count legal king
moves, then account for squares controlled by the opponent.

Space is not simply the number of pawns advanced. It is room in which your pieces can move
safely and usefully. An advanced pawn may gain space while depriving your bishop of a
diagonal. A compact formation can be healthy if the pieces have good routes and a freeing
pawn break. A large formation can become a target if its pawns cannot support each other.

Imagine two armies separated by locked pawns. Bishops may stare at barriers while knights
seek routes around them. Now imagine one central pawn exchange opening a file. Rooks that
seemed irrelevant can suddenly become the most important pieces. The board has not changed
size; the usable network has changed.

Study exercise: take any quiet position from your own play and name one open line, one
blocked line, one useful knight destination, and one square your king must avoid. Then
identify the pawn move most likely to change those relationships. This develops a geometric
understanding that survives changes in opening names and move orders.
