Legal, possible, and wise are different questions
=================================================

A move can follow a piece's movement pattern and still be illegal because it leaves the king
in check. A move can be legal and still lose immediately. A move can be safe for one turn
and still create a lasting weakness. Keep these levels separate. First establish what is
allowed; next ask what the opponent can force; finally judge the longer-term consequences.

The king is never captured in ordinary chess. Check is a warning that must be answered, and
checkmate means that the checked side has no legal escape. Stalemate means the player to
move has no legal move while the king is not in check. This difference explains why a
completely dominant position can suddenly become a draw. When the opponent has almost no
moves, every capture deserves an extra stalemate check.

Responding to check usually means moving the king, capturing the checking piece, or blocking
its line. A knight's check cannot be blocked. When two pieces give check at once, the king
must move. These facts help organize your search, but you still must check the destination
and any newly opened line. Capturing a checking piece with your king is legal only if the
destination is safe.

Ordinary castling moves the king toward a rook and brings that rook across the king. It
requires the relevant pieces to retain their castling rights, a clear intervening path, and
a safe king route. Being able to castle is different from wanting to castle. The side with
fewer advanced pawns may offer better shelter, while the other side may expose the king to
already mobilized attackers. Examine where the king will stand after the move.

Pawns create special practical problems because they cannot retreat. Their forward movement
differs from their diagonal capture, and an eligible passing capture exists only immediately
after the opposing pawn's two-square advance. Promotion allows a queen, rook, bishop, or
knight, regardless of which pieces were previously captured. The queen is usually strongest,
but a knight may give a needed fork and a rook or bishop can occasionally avoid stalemate.

The practical lesson is to rehearse exceptional rules before they appear under time
pressure. Learn the actual requirements for castling, the passing capture, and promotion
using a board or legal-move interface. This library explains their purpose and consequences,
but an event's official rules remain the authority for disputes and claim procedures.

An easy self-test is to explain why each of these statements is unreliable: a pinned piece
does not matter; every check must be answered by moving the king; the strongest promotion is
always the queen; capturing the last pawn must improve a winning position. A pinned piece
can still control a king destination under the attack rules. Checks can often be met by
capture or interposition. Promotion can change stalemate or checking geometry. Removing the
last pawn can eliminate the opponent's final legal move.

Build the habit of asking the legal question before the strategic one. A plan based on an
illegal king move is not an ambitious plan; it is a misread position. Accuracy begins with
respecting the movement and safety constraints that make chess chess.
