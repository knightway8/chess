Playing worse positions without collapsing
==========================================

A worse position is not the same as a lost position. The opponent may have an easier plan,
safer king, better structure, or extra pawn while still needing accurate play. Your job is
to identify what keeps the game alive and avoid creating a second weakness unnecessarily.

Find your best source of activity. It may be a pawn break, a rook file, a passed pawn,
pressure on the king, or a favorable exchange. If pure activity is impossible, improve the
defensive setup and force the opponent to demonstrate a way through.

Do not seek complications at any price. Opening lines can accelerate defeat when your pieces
are less active. Choose complications that activate your army or create a concrete threat.
Randomness favors neither side by itself; positions with difficult choices can increase
practical chances.

Trade according to function. Exchanging the opponent's best piece can ease the defense.
Trading every piece may enter a lost pawn ending. Keeping queens may offer perpetual check,
but it may also expose your king. Calculate the resulting position rather than applying a
blanket rule to trade or avoid trades.

When the opponent has a stable advantage, make them spend time improving. Prevent the
easiest entry, keep the structure flexible, and preserve reserve moves. A passive but solid
setup may be better than a pawn grab that creates a decisive line.

Emotion causes secondary errors. After realizing that an earlier move was bad, players often
rush to 'make up for it' with a sacrifice. The board does not require emotional balance.
Reassess from the current position and choose the move that gives the best remaining
chances.

Set practical objectives: reach the time control, trade the dominant knight, create one safe
checking route, or fix the opponent's pawns on a vulnerable color. These objectives should
serve the position, not distract from immediate tactics.

Exercise: review a loss and mark the point where the position became worse, then the later
point where it became lost. Study the decisions between them. This interval often contains
the most valuable defensive lessons.
