Threat triage and active defense
================================

Defense begins by identifying what is actually threatened. Separate mate, material loss,
structural damage, and mere inconvenience. A threat may have several parts. Saving a rook
while allowing mate is not defense; preventing a harmless pawn capture while missing a queen
entry wastes time.

List forcing resources for the attacker, especially checks, captures, and threats that
remove defenders. Then examine defensive categories: eliminate the attacker, add a defender,
move the target, block the line, trade queens, create an escape, or produce a stronger
counter-threat. Considering categories prevents fixation on the first passive move.

Active defense makes the attacker solve problems. A central pawn break may open lines
against their king. A queen trade may force them to abandon the attack. Returning material
may eliminate the key piece. Activity is not random aggression; it directly changes the
conditions supporting the threat.

Do not defend every pawn. The attacker may be using a small target to pull your pieces away
from the king. Decide what can be given up. Material is a resource, and a pawn can be a
cheap price for coordination or simplification.

Exchange attackers carefully. Removing the queen often reduces mating danger, but the
recapture may open a rook file or create a decisive passer. Trading one attacker may improve
another. Reconstruct the resulting position instead of assuming fewer pieces always means
safety.

When no complete defense exists, choose the line that creates the hardest practical problem.
Seek perpetual-check chances, a fortress, a promotion race, or tactical complications. This
does not change an objectively lost position, but it gives the opponent opportunities to
err.

Avoid despair based on appearance. An advanced enemy queen and rook can look terrifying
while lacking a final entry square. Count legal checks and exits. Conversely, do not relax
because material is equal if your king has no safe answer to the next check.

Exercise: after losing a game to an attack, return to the first defensive decision, not the
final mate. Write three defensive categories you considered and one you missed. The final
blunder is often only the last result of an earlier passive choice.
