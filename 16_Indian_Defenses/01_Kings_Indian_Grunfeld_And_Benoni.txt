King's Indian, Grünfeld, Benoni, and Benko
==========================================

KING'S INDIAN DEFENSE

Black allows White a broad pawn center, develops the king bishop on a long diagonal,
castles, and challenges the center with pawn breaks. In closed main lines, White often
expands on the queenside while Black attacks the king. The pawn chain indicates space, but
timing and king safety decide the race.

The Classical develops naturally and can lead to locked centers. The Sämisch builds a large
supported center and may castle on either side, accepting slower development. The Fianchetto
places White's king bishop on the long diagonal to restrain Black's attack. The Averbakh
develops the queen bishop actively and limits an immediate kingside expansion. Black must
choose the correct break; passive piece placement leaves the space disadvantage without
counterplay.

GRÜNFELD DEFENSE

The Grünfeld invites a large center, then attacks it with the fianchetto bishop, knight
pressure, and a central pawn break. White may have impressive pawns and active pieces, but
the center can become a target. Black needs energetic play and accurate calculation because
conceding space without timely pressure is dangerous.

Exchange structures give White a central pawn mass and Black open lines against it. Quieter
systems preserve tension. White aims to use the center to gain space or launch an attack;
Black seeks to force advances or exchanges that expose its base. The opening is a clear
example of dynamic pressure against occupation.

MODERN BENONI

Black concedes central space and creates an asymmetrical structure with a queenside majority
and a central break. White has a central majority and a stable space advantage. Black seeks
piece activity, pressure on the half-open file, and queenside expansion. The backward pawn
and weak squares are lasting concerns, so activity is essential.

BENKO GAMBIT

Black offers a queenside pawn to open files and diagonals against White's queenside. The
compensation is long-term pressure rather than a short mating attack. White may accept and
try to consolidate, return the pawn for development, or decline. Black's active rooks and
bishop often make the extra pawn difficult to use.

OLD INDIAN AND EAST INDIAN SETUPS

The Old Indian develops the king bishop more modestly behind a pawn and builds a compact
center. Black seeks a freeing break without the immediate long-diagonal pressure of the
King's Indian. These positions reward patient maneuvering but still require counterplay
against White's space.

The shared lesson is that conceding a center creates a debt. Black repays it through timely
breaks and piece pressure. White must convert space into development and useful plans before
the center is undermined.
